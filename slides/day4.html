Suggested structure

Eight slides that tell the whole story

  1. Problem: who has it, why it matters, and your success metric
  2. Data: source, size, labels, languages, splits, limitations
  3. Baseline: the simplest thing that works, with its number
  4. Approach: architecture diagram; why these models
  1. Results: before vs. after table, by slice, with latency and cost
  2. Error analysis: categorized failures, with real examples
  3. Live demo
  4. Lessons and next steps: what you'd do with 2 more weeks
Scoring guide

What each criterion looks like at its best

CriterionWeightStrong evidence
Functional end-to-end solution30%Runs live on unseen input; handles errors, abstains gracefully
Model selection & implementation25%Choice justified by quality and latency/cost; clean, modular code
Quantitative evaluation & error analysis20%Held-out test, baseline, slices (EN/AR), categorized failures
Fine-tuning / justified adaptation15%Before/after on the same test set, or a data-backed case for not tuning
Presentation & reproducibility10%Clear story, on time; README lets a stranger reproduce results
Technical Q&A

Questions you should be ready for

  • Why this model and not a smaller or bigger one? What did you compare?
  • How do you know the test set doesn't leak into training?
  • What's your worst slice, and why?
  • What happens when the model is unsure?
  • How much would this cost at 100k requests per day?
  • How would you detect that it is getting worse in production?
  • Did fine-tuning help? By how much, and is the gap significant?
  • What would break first with real customer data?
Great answers point to a number, a plot or an example from your repo. "We didn't test that, and here's how we would" is a fine answer too.

Patterns across projects

What worked

  • Starting from a simple baseline and keeping it in the results table
  • Evaluating Arabic and English separately
  • Abstaining instead of guessing
  • Cascades: small model first, LLM for the hard tail

What to watch

  • Test sets that overlap with training data
  • Single-run results presented as significant
  • LLM outputs trusted without validation
  • Demos tuned to a few hand-picked examples
